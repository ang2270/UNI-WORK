LM Test for 5’th order ARCH effects LM Test for \(5\)-th order ARCH effects

This is a test for ARCH in levels (conducted at a 5% significance level)

Hypotheses: \[H_0: \text{cov}(\epsilon^2_t, \epsilon^2_{t-j}) = 0, \text{ for } j \neq 0\] \[H_1: \text{at least one } \text{cov}(\epsilon^2_t, \epsilon^2_{t-j}) \neq 0\]

Auxiliary Regression with \(p = 5\): \[\hat{\epsilon}^2_t = \gamma_0 + \gamma_1 \hat{\epsilon}^2_{t-1} + \gamma_2 \hat{\epsilon}^2_{t-2} + \gamma_3 \hat{\epsilon}^2_{t-3} + \gamma_4 \hat{\epsilon}^2_{t-4} + \gamma_5 \hat{\epsilon}^2_{t-5} + v_t\] Tested Hypothesis: \[H_0: \gamma_i = 0, \text{ for all } i = 1, 2,3,4,5 \, \] \[H_1: \text{at least one } \gamma_i \neq 0, \text{ for } i = 1,2,3,4,5 \, \]

Test Statistic: \[W = nR^2\] where \(n\) is the sample size and \(R^2\) is the R-squared of the auxiliary regression.

Distribution under the Null: \(W\) has a \(\chi^2_p\) distribution if \(H_0\) is true. # Residuals: res_d <- residuals(fit_ar1) # Test:

Answer: (a) and (b) The risk measure selected is Value at Risk (VaR) at the 95% confidence level, expressed as a percentage of portfolio value representing the monthly loss threshold. Value at Risk (VaR) is defined as the maximum loss expected to occur in no more than 5% of monthly observations over the sample period. Formally, the 95% VaR is the 5th percentile of the empirical distribution of monthly log-returns. The VaR calculation was performed using the historical 5th percentile of the monthly log-return distribution over the 120 observations, and chosen on the positive side of the distribution. (VaR % therefore expressing a negative loss amount.) I chose to show the VaR of the 9 ETF’s for the following reasons;

Firstly, for risk-adverse investors its an easily understandable and important metric to understand how much one might lose in a serve market draw down. Unlike a measure like standard deviation, which treats all volatility equally, VaR highlights downside tail losses, which for risk-adverse investors serves as a conceptual benchmark to see which ETF’s offer larger drawdowns and losses, which reflects real investor preferences since investors hate losses more than they value gains,

Second, VaR at the 95% confidence level provides a definitive risk metric that translates directly into decision-making: “What is the worst plausible monthly loss I should expect?” This is more intuitive for investors as they can directly see the what kind of risk drawn downs they can expect chasing higher returns, highlighting the idea of “no free lunch”.

Thirdly, equity returns particularly for growth stocks—exhibit fat tails and negative skewness, meaning historical worst-case scenarios are worse than a normal distribution predict. A historical VaR approach captures these tail characteristics. This is particularly relevant for the growth-oriented ETFs (VBK, VOT, VUG) in this analysis, and is shown in the results below: var_box Despite VUG (Large Growth) having the highest mean monthly return from the workshop analysis, it carries an 8.837% VaR, meaning for a $50,000 investment, an investor could lose $4,419 worst-case in one month. In contrast, VTV (Large Value) offers a $3,270 maximum loss in the worst 5% of months, a meaningful difference for risk-averse investors.

Comparing returns and risk across the ETFs reveals a nuanced risk-return relationship that broadly supports the practitioner’s caution, but with important exceptions; Within size categories, a clear positive risk-return trade-off emerges. Large-cap ETFs show returns increasing sharply across styles (Value 0.927%, Blend 1.156%, Growth 1.347%), paired with corresponding VaR increases (6.540%, 7.230%, 8.837%). Mid-cap follows this pattern (Value 0.797%, Growth 0.926%; VaR: 7.422%, 8.499%). However, small-cap presents an anomaly: returns are essentially flat (0.814%–0.836%) while VaR systematically increases with growth orientation (8.534%–9.414%), suggesting small-cap growth provides no return compensation for elevated tail risk. Within style categories, the relationship is more complex. Growth returns increase strongly with size (0.823%, 1.347%), but VaR does not increase monotonically (9.414%, 8.499%, 8.837%), meaning large-cap growth offers superior risk-adjusted returns. Critically, Blend and Value categories show inverse relationships: larger ETFs deliver both higher returns and lower risk (Blend: 0.836%→1.156% returns; 8.595%, 7.230% VaR).
This suggests size diversification reduces idiosyncratic risk without sacrificing returns. Direct comparison of VUG versus alternatives: Despite VUG’s highest mean return (1.347%), it carries 8.837% VaR. A risk-averse investor would rationally prefer VV (Large Blend), which offers 86% of VUG’s return (1.156%) while bearing only 82% of its tail risk (7.230%). Even more striking, VTV (Large Value) with 0.927% return carries merely 6.540% VaR—the lowest in the dataset.
